# AI Field — Experience Thesis

> The experience POV that precedes any visual world. It governs how the product should *feel and behave*, not how it looks. No colors, fonts, or components here. Grounded in `PRODUCT.md`, `plan.md`, and `SPEC.md`; those own product truth. When the visual world is chosen later (DESIGN.md), it must serve this thesis.
>
> **Conceptual read of "Field":** fieldwork, not coursework — a practitioner doing real reps in the field, discovering the terrain of their own capability, and returning to cover new ground. Practice, exploration, missions, capability, progression, discovering gaps, another rep. **Never literal:** no maps, military aesthetics, terrain textures, mission-patch badges, or game HUDs.

---

## 1. Experience thesis

Using AI Field should feel like being handed a real piece of work and trusted to run it yourself. You step into a situation a competent professional would actually face, direct a capable AI toward an outcome you'd put your name on, and then — because someone was watching *how* you worked, not just what you produced — you get the kind of honest, specific debrief a good mentor gives after a real job: here's the move that worked, here's the one you missed, here's how a stronger operator would have run it. Nothing is graded, nothing is gamified, nothing is dressed up as a lesson. Your capability isn't awarded to you; you discover it by doing the work and seeing it reflected back as evidence of what you actually did. The lasting feeling is the one that follows honest fieldwork — you learned something true about how you operate, you know exactly where your edge is, and you already want to run the next one.

## 2. Emotional progression

| Moment | Desired feeling | What it should do to the user |
|---|---|---|
| **Arrival** | *Trusted, not enrolled* | Let in and treated as already capable — handed the keys, not a syllabus. No "level 1," no course to start, no signup gate to cross. |
| **Choosing a mission** | *Oriented, with agency* | You see the range of work you could practice and understand exactly why one rep is the most useful next — a considered recommendation, not a locked rail. You choose; you're not routed. |
| **Briefing** | *Stakes, and a clean threshold* | A deliberate breath before the work: here's the situation, here's what "done" means, here's what you'll hand in. You feel yourself step *into* a piece of work, the small brace before a real task. |
| **Doing the work** | *Absorbed and in command* | Directing, not chatting — deciding what the AI gets, pushing on its answers, building the thing you'll submit. It should feel like operating a capable instrument under your own judgment, quiet and focused, with no coach whispering the answer. |
| **Submitting** | *A real commitment* | Deciding it's good enough is your call, and it should carry the small weight of putting your name on work — not the weightlessness of clicking "next." You commit; then it's out of your hands. |
| **Receiving the debrief** | *Seen, and told the truth* | The sense that someone genuinely watched how you worked and is being straight with you — specific, earned, pitched to you but held to the same bar as everyone. Respect, not praise; coaching, not a score. It lands like a mentor's honest read because it names real moments. |
| **Seeing the next capability gap** | *Productive restlessness* | Not "you leveled up" but "here's the true edge of what you can do, and the one rep that extends it." You leave deliberately incomplete — aware of a specific gap and already wanting to close it. The pull to return is curiosity about your own capability, never a streak. |

The arc overall: **trusted → in command → committed → told the truth → pulled back for another rep.** A practitioner's arc, not a student's.

## 3. Experience principles

Specific enough to reject a design that violates them.

1. **Capability is discovered through evidence, not announced through scores.** Every claim about the user's skill must point to something they actually did. A bar that moves without a visible reason, a numeric grade, a badge, an XP tally, or a level-up celebration is a violation.

2. **The product never takes the wheel.** The interface recommends, frames, and reflects — it never coaches mid-task, auto-completes the user's judgment, or rescues a vague instruction. Suggested prompts in the workbench, "did you mean," inline tips that improve the user's direction, or any element that does the thinking for them is a violation, because it corrupts the measurement.

3. **Every surface feels like entering work, not browsing content.** No screen may present itself as a catalog, a lesson list, a table of records, or a grid of tiles to scan. If a surface reads as "here are things to look at" rather than "here is work to step into," it's wrong.

4. **Honesty over encouragement; respect over praise.** Feedback treats the user as a capable professional owed the truth, not a beginner to be cushioned. Confetti, empty affirmations ("great job!"), motivational streaks, and softened verdicts are violations; specific, earned, sometimes-uncomfortable coaching is the standard.

5. **One clear next rep — and the return is earned, not pressured.** At every ending, the single most useful next action is obvious, and there is only one. The reason to come back is curiosity about a named gap. Competing CTAs, notification-bait, streak counters, and "keep your progress alive" nags are violations.

6. **Show the actual work, not a proxy for it.** The user's own transcript, choices, and deliverable are the material; feedback references real moments and progress mirrors demonstrated behavior. Vanity metrics, aggregate "engagement" stats, fake-precise percentages, and anything that stands in for the real work is a violation.

7. **The concept lives in behavior, not costume.** "Field" and "mission" shape how the experience *behaves* — fieldwork, reps, discovering the terrain of capability — never how it's decorated. Literal maps, insignia, mission-patch badges, terrain textures, or game HUDs are violations. The metaphor earns its place only when it changes an interaction, never as set dressing.

## 4. Product personality

**AI Field is:**

- **Direct** — says the true thing plainly; no hedging, no padding.
- **Composed** — calm, unhurried, sure of itself; weight without noise.
- **Respectful** — treats the user as a capable professional, not a learner to manage.
- **Exacting** — holds a real standard and is specific about what good looks like.
- **Grounded** — practical and real-work; no theater, no hype.

**AI Field deliberately avoids being:**

- **Peppy / gamified** — no cheerleading, mascots, celebrations, or streaks.
- **Patronizing** — never talks down, over-explains, or coddles.
- **Hyped / salesy** — no urgency, superlatives, or "unlock / level up" language.
- **Bureaucratic** — no LMS, course-portal, or compliance-training tone; no modules-and-forms feel.
- **Sterile / analytical** — grounded and human, never a cold metrics readout or dashboard detachment.

(The paired tension to hold: *composed* without tipping into *sterile*; *direct* without tipping into *cold*.)

---

## 5. Signature product idea

**What a user remembers after one session:** *"Something finally judged how I direct AI — not just what it produced — and was honest enough to name the exact skill I was missing and the one rep that would build it."*

The thing they carry isn't a score or a screen; it's **the debrief** — the slightly uncomfortable, clearly-earned sense of having been watched fairly by an expert and told the truth about how they operate, then walking away already knowing their next rep. Everything else in the product exists to make that one moment land and feel deserved. If a user forgets everything else, they should remember that AI Field is the place that told them the truth about how they work with AI.
